\subsection{Gate Transmission Efficiency}

To accurately reconstruct the ionization yield of the thermalized \isotope{Ra}{224} recoils, the
electron transmission efficiency (also referred to as grid transparency) of the gate electrode must
be precisely characterized. This efficiency dictates the fraction of ionization electrons that
successfully pass from the drift region into the electroluminescence (EL) gap to produce the
secondary scintillation signal.

The transmission measurement was performed using the exact detector geometry as the primary
experiment—the same chamber with a drift gap of 1.4 cm and an EL gap of 0.8 cm delimited by
identical gate and anode meshes. However, to generate a macroscopic and stable direct current (DC)
suitable for electrometer readings, the active silicon PIN detector and \isotope{Th}{228} source
were replaced with a passive 50 kHz \isotope{Cf}{252} source. The high emission rate of the
\isotope{Cf}{252} source provided a continuous, uniform flux of ionizing radiation throughout the
active volume.

The DC current was continuously monitored on the gate electrode ($I_{\text{gate}}$) using a
precision electrometer (Keithley 6514). The measurement relies on normalizing the current against a
zero-transmission baseline. By setting the anode voltage to zero ($V_{\text{anode}} = 0$), the
electric field in the EL gap is nullified ($E_{\text{EL}} = 0$). Under this condition, all
electrons drifting towards the gate are collected by it, providing the normalization current
$I_{0}$. The stability of this baseline across varying cathode voltages is demonstrated in
\ref{fig:apx_transmission}a confirming complete electron drift saturation and the validity of the
$I_0$ measurement.

As the anode voltage is increased and the EL field is established, a fraction of the drifting
electrons is extracted through the gate mesh and into the EL gap. This results in a measurable
depletion of the gate current (\ref{fig:apx_transmission}b). The gate transmission efficiency $T$
is therefore directly determined by this depletion relative to the baseline:

$$T = 1 - \frac{I_{\text{gate}}}{I_{0}}$$

Measurements were taken across a wide range of cathode, gate, and anode voltages to map the
transmission efficiency. According to standard electrostatic models of wire meshes, electron
transparency should scale primarily with the ratio of the fields on either side of the grid, $R =
E_{\text{drift}} / E_{\text{EL}}$. The experimental data confirms this primary scaling behavior,
though it reveals a persistent secondary dependence on the absolute drift field. When parameterized
by the ratio $R$, the transmission data forms a structured family of curves
(\ref{fig:apx_transmission}c). To accurately capture this behavior, the data was parameterized
using a ``dynamic" sigmoid function. In this empirical model, the standard sigmoid
coefficients—namely the steepness ($k$) and the inflection point ($x_0$)—are not fixed constants,
but are instead evaluated as continuous functions of $E_{\text{drift}}$. This approach seamlessly
bridges the individual curves, yielding a complete 2D analytical surface for the gate transmission
across the entire operational parameter space (\ref{fig:apx_transmission}d). This transparency
surface is used throughout the primary data analysis to systematically correct the measured charge
yields for any given combination of drift and EL fields.

This universal relationship was successfully parameterized using a dynamic sigmoid function,
yielding a continuous, 2D analytical model for the gate transmission across the entire operational

\subsection{Gamma Spectroscopy and \isotope{Ra}{224} Desorption Efficiency}

To quantify the efficiency of \isotope{Ra}{224} recoiling out of the \isotope{Th}{228} source and
implanting into the aluminum catcher foil, the collected activity was independently measured using
a Hidex AMG gamma spectrometer. The foil was exposed to the source under vacuum for accumulation
periods ranging from 5 to 9 days, after which it was transferred to the spectrometer to monitor the
subsequent radioactive decay.

\subsubsection{Spectral Analysis and Peak Fitting}
The primary signature used for this analysis is the prominent $\sim$240 keV gamma peak, which is a
convolution of the 241.0 keV emission from \isotope{Ra}{224} ($4.1\%$ branching ratio) and the
238.6 keV emission from its daughter \isotope{Pb}{212} ($43.6\%$ branching ratio). For each
discrete scan, the region of interest was fitted with a Gaussian profile atop a linear background
using strictly Poisson-weighted error minimization to extract the net area
(\ref{fig:apx_gamma_spec} a).

    \caption{\isotope{Ra}{224} desorption efficiency measurement using gamma spectroscopy.
        \textbf{(a)} Example gamma spectrum with the fit to the Ra peak.
        \textbf{(b)} Bateman decay curve for one of the measurements.
        \textbf{(c)} Bar chart summarizing accumulated activities vs. theoretical maximum capacities.
    }
    \label{fig:apx_gamma_spec}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Decay Kinetics and Bateman Modeling}
Because the $\sim$240 keV peak is dominated by the high-branching-ratio \isotope{Pb}{212} daughter,
the observed count rate evolves dynamically as \isotope{Pb}{212} grows into transient equilibrium
with the parent \isotope{Ra}{224}, before ultimately decaying with the 3.63-day half-life of
\isotope{Ra}{224}. To disentangle the populations, the time-series net count rates were fitted to
the coupled Bateman equations (\ref{fig:apx_gamma_spec} b).

By leaving the initial \isotope{Pb}{212} population ($N_{\text{Pb,0}}$) as a free parameter, the
fit intrinsically diagnoses the loss of the intermediate \isotope{Rn}{220} gas. The fits
consistently indicated near-zero or negative initial \isotope{Pb}{212} populations when
back-propagated, physically confirming that the vast majority of the \isotope{Rn}{220} escapes the
foil during the vacuum accumulation and subsequent transfer phases.

\subsubsection{Saturation Capacity and Desorption Efficiency}
The extracted $t=0$ populations were decay-corrected backwards across the transfer delay to
determine the exact number of accumulated \isotope{Ra}{224} atoms at the end of the vacuum
exposure. Because the foils were exposed for finite durations (achieving $60\% - 82\%$ of total
saturation), these values were extrapolated to their theoretical absolute saturation capacity,
$\Gamma_{\text{Ra, max}}$, where the recoil collection rate exactly equals the decay rate
(\ref{fig:apx_gamma_spec} c).

To determine the absolute desorption probability ($P_{\text{desorp}}$), the saturation capacity of
the foil ($\Gamma_{\text{Ra, max}} \approx 18.3$ Bq) was compared to the true activity of the
\isotope{Th}{228} source. To circumvent severe data acquisition (DAQ) dead-time issues discovered
in high-speed waveform digitizers, the source activity was independently measured using
hardware-binned Multi-Channel Analyzer (MCA) alpha spectroscopy, yielding a decay-corrected ground
truth of $\Gamma_{\text{Th}} \approx 45.7$ Bq at the time of the gamma measurements.

Assuming the aluminum foil subtends approximately half of the $4\pi$ solid angle
($\epsilon_{\text{geom}} \approx 0.5$), the desorption probability is given by:

$$P_{\text{desorp}} = \frac{\Gamma_{\text{Ra, max}}}{\Gamma_{\text{Th}} \cdot \epsilon_{\text{geom}}}$$

Using the measured values, we find a desorption probability of $P_{\text{desorp}} \approx 80\%$.
This high efficiency confirms that the \isotope{Th}{228} source is highly diluted and sparse,
approaching a monolayer. The vast majority of \isotope{Ra}{224} atoms directed upward successfully
escape the source substrate without being fully attenuated by self-absorption, validating this
setup as a highly efficient mechanism for \isotope{Ra}{224} tagging.

\subsection{Geometric Acceptance Correction for X-ray measurements}

To accurately calibrate the S2 light yield of $^{224}\text{Ra}$ recoils using 12.3 keV X-rays, it
is necessary to account for the disparity in their spatial distributions. The recoils are emitted
from a highly localized 5 mm radius source at the center of the cathode and possess a negligible
penetration range in the gas. Consequently, their primary ionization tracks are produced strictly
on-axis. In contrast, the 12.3 keV X-rays are emitted isotropically and have a mean free path of
$\lambda \approx$ 2.5 cm in Xenon at 2 bar, leading to interaction vertices distributed throughout
the drift volume ($r <$ 6.0 cm, $z <$ 1.4 cm).

Because the photomultiplier tube (PMT) subtends a smaller solid angle for off-axis events, an
uncorrected X-ray calibration would underestimate the true light yield at the center of the
detector. To quantify this effect, a Monte Carlo simulation was developed to map the relative
geometric detection efficiency.

The simulation models isotropic emission from the cathode source disk and propagates the X-rays
using the photoelectric attenuation length. For each valid interaction within the drift region, the
relative solid angle $\Omega \propto 1/d^2$ is calculated from the center of the
electroluminescence (EL) gap to the PMT window. These efficiencies are normalized to the average
acceptance of the centralized recoils.

The results of this simulation are illustrated in \ref{fig:apx_mc_xrays}.

The simulation yields a global correction factor of $G_{ratio} = 0.89$. This indicates that, due to
the "pancak" geometry of the active volume, the spatial spread of the X-rays introduces
approximately an \textbf{11\%} geometric penalty. The measured average X-ray S2 response must therefore be scaled
by $1 / G_{ratio}$ to derive the expected standard-candle response for an on-axis event, allowing for an accurate calculation of the recoil quenching factor.
