\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Ionization yield and recombination}

The primary objective of this experimental campaign is to characterize the electron-ion
recombination dynamics of heavy nuclear recoils in high-pressure xenon. When traversing a noble
gas, the recombination fraction of the generated electron-ion pairs is fundamentally governed by
the localized ionization density ($dE/dx$) of the particle track. Heavy nuclear recoils produce
exceptionally dense charge topologies compared to lighter or minimum ionizing particles, leading to
a massive increase in the local Coulomb attraction between thermalized electrons and their parent
ions.

To extract a measurable electron yield, the applied drift field must be sufficient to overpower
this dense recombination process. Figure~\ref{fig:collection_eff} illustrates this striking
disparity by comparing the normalized charge collection efficiency of \isotope{Ra}{224} recoils
against established literature values for alpha particles and beta electrons
\cite{saito_simultaneous_2003, bolotnikov_spectroscopic_1997, henriques_understanding_2024} as a
function of the reduced drift field. While the charge yield for alphas and betas approaches
saturation at modest reduced fields between $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-1}$~kV/cm/bar, the heavy nuclear
recoils require drift fields approximately three orders of magnitude larger to overcome the severe
track recombination and reach a comparable extraction plateau.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/collect_effs_alpha_beta_recoils.png}
    \caption{Normalized charge collection efficiency as a function of reduced drift field. The severe recombination within the dense \isotope{Ra}{224} recoil tracks requires drift fields approximately three orders of magnitude higher to achieve saturation compared to alpha particles and beta electrons (literature data adapted from \cite{saito_simultaneous_2003, bolotnikov_spectroscopic_1997, henriques_understanding_2024}).}
    \label{fig:collection_eff}
\end{figure}

To quantitatively map this saturation behavior for our specific active source,
Figure~\ref{fig:golden_plot} presents the measured secondary scintillation (S2) area as a function
of the applied drift field for \isotope{Th}{228} and \isotope{Ra}{224} decays. The data was
acquired across five distinct amplification settings ($\Delta V_{\text{THGEM}}$) to rigorously
evaluate the instrumental response. The raw S2 areas, displayed in a logarithmic scale in
Figure~\ref{fig:golden_plot}(a,b), illustrate the expected field-dependent signature: at lower
drift fields, recombination severely suppresses the extractable charge, while increasing the field
drives a monotonic rise in the extraction efficiency.

To isolate the intrinsic gas physics from the instrumental gain, the S2 areas for each run were
normalized to their respective maximum values, as shown in linear scale in
Figure~\ref{fig:golden_plot}(c,d). This normalization collapses the diverse $\Delta
V_{\text{THGEM}}$ datasets onto a single, consistent saturation curve. This uniform behavior
confirms that the observed charge yield dynamics are an intrinsic property of the xenon gas and the
recoil track topology, completely decoupled from the linear amplification response of the THGEM.

The normalized curves demonstrate that the collection efficiency for these heavy recoils reaches a
stable plateau between 1500~V/cm and 2000~V/cm. Furthermore, at extreme drift fields approaching
3000~V/cm, a slight upward inflation in the normalized S2 area is observed. This deviation marks
the threshold where the effective field in the drift gap begins to generate its own mild
electroluminescence, a secondary effect that will be modeled and discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Finally, beyond the general saturation trend, the absolute ionization yield differs markedly
between the distinct isotopes of the decay chain. Comparing the asymptotic limits in
Figure~\ref{fig:golden_plot}(a) and \ref{fig:golden_plot}(b), the \isotope{Th}{228} decays
systematically produce smaller S2 areas than the subsequent \isotope{Ra}{224} decays, hinting at a
strong mass and energy dependence that warrants a broader isotopic comparison.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{golden_plot.png}
    \caption{S2 area saturation curves for heavy nuclear recoils as a function of the applied drift field. (a, b) Raw S2 areas for \isotope{Th}{228} and \isotope{Ra}{224} decays, plotted on a logarithmic y-axis to accommodate the large dynamic range across different $\Delta V_{\text{THGEM}}$ settings. (c, d) The same datasets normalized to their respective maximum values, plotted on a linear y-axis. The collapse of the data onto a unified curve demonstrates that charge collection plateaus between 1500 and 2000~V/cm, independent of the THGEM gain.}

\subsection{Isotope-specific yield and structural deviation}

Building upon the saturation dynamics established across the \isotope{Th}{228} and
\isotope{Ra}{224} decays, we extend the analysis to the full accessible decay chain.
Figure~\ref{fig:isotope_s2} presents the centroid S2 area for all six primary recoil
species—\isotope{Th}{228}, \isotope{Ra}{224}, \isotope{Rn}{220}, \isotope{Bi}{212},
\isotope{Po}{216}, and \isotope{Po}{212}—as a function of the drift field at a fixed amplification
setting ($\Delta V_{\text{THGEM}} = 800$~V).

While all isotopes exhibit the characteristic low-field recombination and high-field saturation
plateau, their absolute S2 yields differ significantly. This variance is driven by the distinct
initial kinetic energies of the recoiling daughters. For instance, the highly energetic 146~keV
\isotope{Pb}{208} recoil (produced by the \isotope{Po}{212} alpha decay) generates a substantially
longer and less dense ionization track than the 84~keV \isotope{Ra}{224} recoil. This lower $dE/dx$
proportionally reduces the electron-ion recombination probability, allowing a greater fraction of
the primary charge to escape the track and produce a larger S2 signal.

To evaluate whether these measured yields align with standard kinematic expectations, we compare
the relative S2 areas against the theoretical energy partitioning. According to Lindhard theory,
only a fraction of a recoiling ion's kinetic energy is channeled into electronic excitation
(ionization), while the remainder is lost to nuclear quenching (phonon generation).
Figure~\ref{fig:expected_vs_measured} illustrates this comparison.

In Figure~\ref{fig:expected_vs_measured}(a), the measured S2 areas are normalized to the
\isotope{Th}{228} response and plotted against the theoretical ratio of electronic energy
deposition. If the ionization yield scaled perfectly with the Lindhard prediction across all
species, the data would follow a linear 1:1 relation. Instead, we observe a systematic divergence;
as we move down the decay chain to higher-energy recoils (such as \isotope{Po}{216} and
\isotope{Po}{212}), the measured S2 ratio significantly exceeds the theoretical expectation.
Figure~\ref{fig:expected_vs_measured}(b) displays this same trend in absolute S2 area.

This structural deviation suggests that standard Lindhard partitioning, while broadly applicable,
may underestimate the electronic stopping power for these highly energetic, heavy nuclear recoils
in xenon gas, resulting in a higher-than-expected charge extraction efficiency once the
recombination regime is saturated.
