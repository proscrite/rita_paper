\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Precise measurements of ionization and electroluminescence signals in high-pressure xenon are essential for the development of next-generation rare-event detectors and for techniques such as ion tagging (``Ba‑tagging'') that promise powerful background rejection. The RITA (Radium Ion Tagging) setup was designed to explore the physics and engineering challenges of producing, tagging and transporting single ions in a high-pressure xenon environment. In RITA, an alpha‑emitting coating (\isotope{Th}{228}) on a silicon detector provides a coincident tag: the emitted alpha is stopped in the silicon while the recoiling daughter ion (e.g. \isotope{Ra}{224} or its descendants) is ejected into the xenon gas where it produces ionization and electroluminescence signals.

The RITA apparatus was designed with a specific downstream application in mind: development and characterization of single‑ion capture methods and sensing approaches relevant to barium‑tagging. By selecting only the alpha energy window that corresponds to \isotope{Ra}{224} recoils and by switching the gate bias on the timescale of the recoil transit, RITA produces a narrow, event‑by‑event stream of \Rapp\ ions directed at a localized sensor surface. Because radium and barium are chemically analogous, \Rapp\ serves as a surrogate to test capture approaches, chemical interactions with fluorescent molecules, molecule immobilization strategies, and single‑molecule fluorescence readout systems that would be used for Ba‑tagging in larger detectors.


The gate/grid therefore acts as an electronic mass/identity filter: the silicon alpha signal is converted to a digital accept/reject decision by the SCA (the ROI excludes alpha energies that overlap with other daughters, notably the Th satellite that overlaps Ra). When an accepted alpha is detected the fast high‑voltage switch briefly changes the gate potential to permit transit of the positively charged daughter into the EL/extraction region and toward the sensor; otherwise the gate remains in its nominal (blocking) state and unwanted daughters are prevented from reaching the sensor. This timed, per‑event selection is the core operating principle that allows RITA to study single‑ion delivery and downstream capture efficiency.

Note that the schematic depicts a conceptual downstream sensor surface for clarity of the per‑ion delivery concept; the present RITA implementation does not include a dedicated sensor plane. In our apparatus the PMT is located at that plane and serves as the primary optical readout rather than a separate sensor surface.

One important physical effect in dense noble gases is electron–ion recombination: a fraction of the ionization electrons produced by a recoil recombine with ions before they can be extracted and converted into electroluminescence (S2). Recombination reduces the detected electron yield and depends on the local ionization density, the applied drift field, and other detector-specific conditions (EL field, gas pressure, collection efficiency). Understanding recombination is therefore crucial both for interpreting observed S2 signals and for optimizing ion‑extraction techniques in tagging applications.

This paper presents an initial, quantitative characterization of electron recombination measured with the RITA setup. We focus on recoil species produced by the \isotope{Th}{228} decay chain — in particular the recoiling \isotope{Ra}{224} and \isotope{Rn}{220} nuclei — and we study how the detected electron yield (and the inferred recombination fraction) depends on the applied drift field and the EL field. A complementary, detector‑level calibration is obtained from dedicated X‑ray runs: the mean S2 area of the 12.3 keV X‑ray is used to compute a gain factor $g_{\mathrm{S2}}$ that converts S2 area (mV·µs) to detected electrons.

In addition to the primary recombination measurements, this manuscript documents several supporting experimental efforts that help interpret the observed yields: (A) a gate transmission and collection‑efficiency study, and (B) a neutralization/collection measurement where recoiling radium species are physically collected and assayed by gamma spectroscopy to estimate charge‑state survival in vacuum versus xenon. These auxiliary experiments provide independent constraints on how many ions are expected to reach the extraction region and how much neutralization may reduce the number of extractable ions.
